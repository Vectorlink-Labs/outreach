The idea

What we're building

A tool that finds real conversations where someone has the problem our product solves — and tells us which ones are worth replying to.

Today, finding these conversations means someone manually searching GitHub and Hacker News, reading dozens of threads, and guessing which are worth the effort. That takes hours and most of it is wasted on results that turn out to be irrelevant.

This tool does the searching and the reading. A marketer fills in a short form describing what they want. The system searches the sources, sorts everything by how strong an opportunity it is, and for the best results explains what the person's problem is, whether they're actively looking for a solution, and how well our product fits. Every result links back to the original post, so nothing has to be taken on trust.

The output isn't 500 links. It's 20 ranked conversations with a reason attached to each one.

Since the first draft

What changed, and why

Four decisions changed the shape of the product. All four make it simpler to build and easier to explain.

AreaFirst draftNowWhy it's better
Interface Chatbot A form A saved form can be re-run automatically every night and show only what's new. A chat message can't. This is the single biggest gain.
Workflow engine LangGraph Plain Python The steps run in a fixed order that never changes. A framework for branching logic adds nothing when there's no branching.
Tool protocol MCP Direct API calls MCP lets an AI assistant discover and call tools. Nothing here needs discovering — our code calls GitHub and Hacker News directly.
The promise "We crawl the internet" "Connected public sources" Accurate, legally safer, and it lets us add sources over time without changing the story.
In short

Less software to build, less to maintain, and a product that does something the chatbot version couldn't: watch the market continuously instead of answering one question at a time.

The difficult parts

Three ideas that make it work

Most of this system is ordinary software. Three things are where the product is won or lost.

1. Show the expanded keywords before spending anything

The user types four keywords. We turn them into fifteen. If we do that silently and the expansion is wrong, they get bad results and blame the product. So we show them the expanded list, mark which words came from them and which from the AI, and let them delete anything they don't like — alongside a live estimate of how many results they're likely to get.

We also show three or four example posts the system is looking for — written out in full. Nobody can review a search algorithm, but anyone can read "I updated a document and my search still returns the old version. Do I have to rebuild everything?" and say "yes, that's exactly it" or "no, that's not what I meant." It takes fifteen seconds and it's the difference between a trusted tool and a black box.

2. Search wide, not just for the keywords

This is easy to get wrong and expensive to fix later. Meaning-based search can only re-sort posts we already downloaded. If we only download posts containing our keywords, the post that describes the problem in completely different words was never downloaded, and no amount of clever ranking will find it.

So the crawl itself has to cast a wider net: the expanded phrases, plus whole pools of posts — every recent issue in the five or six repositories where our buyers already are. In a pool like that, every post is a candidate and no keyword is needed at all.

3. Sort cheaply first, think expensively later

The order of steps 5 and 6 is the difference between a 20-cent search and a $2.40 search for identical results. Sort first with the cheap specialist model, then let the expensive AI read only what survived. Getting this backwards is the most common way a system like this becomes too expensive to run.

The number

How results are scored

Every result gets one score out of 100, built from six signals:

35%Are they actively looking?Asking for a solution beats just complaining
20%How bad is the problemCosting real money beats mild annoyance
15%Are they the right kind of personA developer in a relevant project, not a bot
15%How recentA live thread beats a dead one
15%How much attention it hasEnough eyes to matter, not so many we'd be buried
Fix

Relevance is not one of the six — it's a gate. In the first draft, relevance was worth 30% and the other five were worth 70%. That means a wildly irrelevant post that happened to be popular and recent scored 67 out of 100 — the same band as a perfect match. Users judge the whole tool by the worst thing on the first page, so this one detail would have sunk it.

Now relevance multiplies everything else. That same junk post scores 1. A genuinely relevant older post that used to score 72 now scores 57 and sits correctly above it.

The last mile

Drafting a reply

When a result is worth answering, the user clicks Draft a reply and gets a suggested response: a genuinely useful answer first, and only then an optional mention of our product.

Two rules, and both are deliberate:

  • Only on click, never for every result. Drafting all 20 would add about 40 seconds to every search for drafts nobody reads. Most searches produce two or three worth answering.
  • A person edits it and posts it themselves. The tool never posts anything anywhere. This isn't caution for its own sake — an obvious bot dropping product mentions in developer threads gets flagged fast and damages the brand we're marketing. Reddit's terms explicitly forbid using their API to spam.

What we're deliberately not using

LangGraph The steps never branch. A framework for building workflows where an AI decides what happens next earns its cost only when an AI actually decides something. Here the order is fixed before the search starts. Replacing it: about forty lines of ordinary Python.
MCP Nothing needs to discover anything. MCP lets an AI assistant find and call tools it doesn't own. Our own code calls GitHub and Hacker News directly. Worth revisiting only as a future feature — letting a customer's own AI assistant query our data.
LangChain Same reasoning. We need one small function that sends text to a model and validates what comes back. That's a short file, not a dependency.
A separate search engine Not yet. PostgreSQL handles both kinds of search at our size. We'll add Elasticsearch or similar only if we measure a real need — and we'll know, because we'll be measuring.

Sequence

Build order

Every milestone ends with something demonstrable, so progress is visible from week one.

Week 0

Prove the leads exist — before writing any code

Hand-run ten searches in a browser. Paste twenty results each into a spreadsheet. Have a marketer grade them: is this a real opportunity, yes or no?

If fewer than three per search are genuinely good, the problem is the premise, not the engineering — and we've spent two days finding out instead of two months. If it passes, we've also just built the first third of our quality benchmark.

The two connectors, the database, cleanup and duplicate removal. No interface yet.

Proof

How we'll know it's working

Three separate questions. They fail independently, so we track them separately — a search that finishes in eight seconds and returns rubbish passes every speed check.

Question 1

Is it fast and reliable?

Straightforward engineering measures, on a dashboard from week one.

  • First result on screen under 8s
  • Search completes under 90s
  • Searches that succeed over 98%
  • Cost per search under $0.35

Question 2

Are the results any good?

Needs a benchmark: 30 real searches with their results graded by hand, once. About one day's work, and nothing improves without it.

  • Good posts we found at all 95%
  • Top 10 that are useful 80%
  • Searches with junk in the top 5 under 5%

Question 3

Is it worth anything?

The only question that matters to the business. Measured from what users actually do.

  • Results opened per search
  • Results marked "not useful"
  • Replies drafted
  • Replies actually posted

The benchmark is the part that's easy to skip and expensive to skip. Without 30 hand-graded searches, every change to the ranking is a guess, and nobody can tell an improvement from a regression. With it, every change is checked automatically before it ships — and the work is one person for one day, reusing the spreadsheet from Week 0.

The strongest single measure is the last one: replies actually posted per week. It's the only number that can't be gamed by making one part look good at another's expense.

The single biggest saving is storing the AI's reading of each post and reusing it. The same conversation gets read once and reused across every search and every customer — which is why a nightly monitor costs two cents instead of eleven. Hosting on top of this is small: one database, one Redis, two small containers.
